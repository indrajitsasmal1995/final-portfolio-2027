Three calls I reversed

Each first version made sense until someone used it.

Decision 01

How should an answer read?

FirstOne continuous reply

NowFive labelled parts, every time

Options we looked at
One continuous reply; a short summary that links to the manual; five labelled parts.
Why I changed it
Watching 4 engineers work in the platform, I saw them read the whole reply to find the one step they needed. The client’s manuals showed how to split it into parts they could jump to.
What it cost
A fixed template. A simple fault still gets all five parts, so some answers read longer than they need to.
Decision 02

Where should an admin fix an answer?

FirstIn a side panel

NowOn a full page, fix beside the thread

Options we looked at
A side panel beside the inbox; a modal over it; a full page with the fix beside the thread.
Why I changed it
Question, answer, report, conversation and fix form were stacked in one narrow column, so finding what went wrong meant scrolling past everything else.
What it cost
Leaving the inbox. Working through a run of flags now means going back and forth, where a panel let the admin skim them in place.
Decision 03

How should adoption be counted?

Options we looked at
Engineers who have ever used it; weekly active engineers; the share using it on an average day.
First
Engineers who have ever used it.
Now
The share of engineers using it on an average day.
Why I changed it
“Ever used” only climbs, so it can’t show a pilot losing interest. The daily share is the number that moves.
What it cost
A smaller, less flattering number in pilot reviews, and one that dips on quiet days, so the tile has to explain it.

A fault, start to finish

Five moments from one fault, from the first question to the fix every later engineer gets.

  1. 01Ask
  2. 02Read the procedure
  3. 03Check the answer
  4. 04Flag or resolve
  5. 05Fix it for everyone
Step 01

Ask

A greeting, one field, and the scope one tap away. While it works, it says what it’s doing in five status lines instead of one spinner, and a question that’s off-topic or too thin gets a reply asking for the platform, part or code.

Step 02

Read the procedure

What’s wrong, what to make safe first, then short checkable steps in order. A procedure with branches can be drawn as a flowchart in one tap, still with its source and confidence.

Step 03

Check the answer

The footer is where trust is earned: the sources, and a confidence score explained in plain words. It shows how closely the documents match the question, lowered by details still missing.

Step 04

Flag or resolve

An engineer says what was wrong in their own words, and it goes straight to the admin inbox. Resolving closes the chat and asks for a category, so every resolution rate in the console stays true.

Step 05

Fix it for everyone

The flag lands in the inbox, sorted by type and timed against a two-day target. On the record, the fix sits beside the whole thread. Here the copilot had quoted 18 N·m from a superseded revision, and the fix points later answers to Rev G.

Three questions an admin asks every week

The console is for the people behind the answers, built around the three things they need to know.

Question 01

Is it being used?

The overview opens on activity by day, then five numbers: adoption, conversations, resolution rate, feedback coverage and response time. Each tile says how its number is worked out, because a number an admin can’t check is one they won’t trust.

Question 02

Are the answers good?

Every conversation is listed with its status, confidence and sources, and opens exactly as the engineer saw it, with what went into the answer beside it.

Question 03

Where is the knowledge failing?

The knowledge base shows every approved document and whether it indexed. The feedback inbox is where a flagged answer starts on its way to a fix.

What I learned

  1. An honest confidence score earns more trust than a confident tone.So the score says why it is lower, not just how low.
  2. Correcting an answer needs the whole conversation in view.So the fix got a page of its own.
  3. The way back from a wrong answer mattered more than any single screen.Every flag makes the next answer better.
  4. Test the format before you settle it.The long reply only showed its cost at the tool. Next time a rough answer goes in front of engineers first.
